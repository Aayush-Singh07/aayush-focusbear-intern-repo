# Inclusive Design Reflection — Aayush Kumar Singh

## Research & Learn

**Who are considered vulnerable populations, and challenges they face in digital spaces:**
Vulnerable populations in a digital/product context include neurodivergent individuals (ADHD, Autism), people with mental health conditions, people with physical or cognitive disabilities, older adults less familiar with technology, and others who may struggle with standard UX assumptions. Common challenges include sensory overload from cluttered interfaces, difficulty with unpredictable navigation, cognitive overload from too much information at once, and tools that assume a level of executive functioning (planning, prioritizing, initiating tasks) that doesn't match how their brain actually works.

**Ethical considerations when designing or working with neurodivergent individuals:**
- Avoid overwhelming UX — too many options, notifications, or visual elements at once increases cognitive load rather than helping
- Respect sensory needs — harsh colors, excessive animation, or sudden changes can be genuinely distressing, not just mildly annoying
- Ensure clear, unambiguous communication — vague instructions or implied expectations create unnecessary friction
- Avoid shame-based framing (e.g., guilt-driven streak-breaking notifications) since many neurodivergent users already carry significant frustration around "failed" productivity systems

**Making interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism:**
- Simple, direct language over long or ambiguous copy
- Predictable, consistent navigation patterns (don't rearrange things unexpectedly)
- Reducing cognitive load by breaking tasks into small, clear steps rather than large abstract goals
- Giving users control over sensory intensity (animation, sound, notification frequency)

**Supporting neurodivergent team members in a professional setting:**
- Be explicit and clear in communication rather than relying on implied context
- Respect different working styles — some people need more processing time, some prefer written over verbal instructions
- Allow flexibility in schedule/environment where possible, rather than assuming one working pattern fits everyone
- Avoid making assumptions about someone's capability based on how they communicate or work

## Task 1: First-Person Account

I read a first-person blog post from an ADHD and product management coach ("Tech Atypically" on Substack) reflecting on their own experience trying productivity tools. A key point that stood out: <cite index="23-1">their core takeaway was that believing the right app or tool alone would solve their ADHD-related struggles was a false belief — the reality is that tools only work well once someone understands their own behavior patterns and picks tools that actually fit their personal style, rather than expecting a tool to fix things by itself.</cite> They mentioned personally trying multiple productivity apps and AI tools in a short span with limited success, which reinforced that the tool itself isn't the core solution — the fit between the tool and the person's actual behavior is.

**Why this matters for Focus Bear specifically:** this reframes the product's job — it's not just "give users a tool," it's "help users build genuine self-awareness and a system that fits how they actually function," which is a much higher bar than typical productivity apps aim for.

## Task 2: One Accessibility Improvement I'd Suggest
Based on the reading above, one concrete idea: instead of a single generic onboarding flow, Focus Bear could ask a few short, low-effort questions early on to surface a user's actual patterns (e.g., when they tend to lose focus, what kind of reminders feel supportive vs. stressful) and use that to lightly tailor the initial experience — directly addressing the "one-size-fits-all tool doesn't work" problem instead of assuming every ADHD/Autistic user needs the same defaults.

## Task 3: Sample Response to a Struggling User

*Hypothetical user message: "I keep opening the app, setting a task, and then just... not doing it. I don't know what's wrong with me."*

**My response:**
"That's a really common experience, and it doesn't mean anything is wrong with you — starting a task is often the hardest part for ADHD brains specifically, even when you genuinely want to do it. It's not about willpower. A couple of things that sometimes help: breaking the task down into something smaller than it currently feels (even just 'open the file' instead of 'finish the report'), or pairing it with a very short focus session so it feels less open-ended. You don't have to fix this all at once — even noticing the pattern like you just did is a real step forward."

## Reflection

**Adjusting communication style to be more inclusive:**
Being more explicit and direct rather than assuming shared context, avoiding jargon or ambiguous phrasing, and giving people space/time to process instead of expecting instant responses.

**Common UX or communication pitfalls that could hurt accessibility:**
Overly cheerful or guilt-driven notification copy, cluttered screens with too many simultaneous actions, and assuming a single onboarding flow fits every user's actual needs — all things that could make Focus Bear feel like just another generic productivity app instead of one that's actually built differently.

**One practical change I can make in my own work:**
When building or reviewing UI, actively ask "does this add cognitive load that isn't necessary?" before adding any new element, animation, or notification — defaulting to removing friction rather than adding features, since for this specific user base, less is often genuinely more.
